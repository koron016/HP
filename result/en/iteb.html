You read the room, work through people, and set things up before they are needed. Not leading exactly — clearing the way so everyone else can move.

Your own work is always the last thing left.

Strengths

  • Sees the logistics of the whole thing without trying
  • Makes it easy for everyone else to move
  • Catches what's been forgotten, first

Watch out for

  • You absorb too much of other people's work
  • Your own deliverables slide to last

Where you do your best work

Work that spans people and departments — programme management, operations, coordination.

A line for your résumé

With a lot of moving parts, I get the logistics in order first so everyone involved can just get on with their piece.

Written so you can drop it straight into an application or interview answer. Adapt it to your own experience.

Interview prep

Answering “what's your weakness?”

  • I moved on instinct and struggled when asked to justify it. Now I write the reasoning down before I decide.
  • I leaned on others before thinking it through. Now I bring my own draft first, then ask.
  • I planned as if nothing would go wrong. Now I always build in slack.
  • I had views but was slow to voice them. Now I make myself speak at least once in every meeting.

What they will press you on

  • “What was that decision actually based on?” → Even for gut calls, have one reason you can put into words afterwards
  • “What have you achieved on your own?” → Inside the group story, cut out the part that was specifically yours
  • “What did you do when the plan fell apart?” → Have one story ready where the plan did not hold
  • “Tell me about a time you took the initiative.” → From the support story, extract one moment that was your own call

What to make the centre of your story

  • Speed off the mark is your edge — make “why I could move immediately” the centre
  • How many people you moved, and how — numbers land here
  • The preparation phase — very few applicants write about it
  • How the person you supported changed — write it through their change

A weakness lands when you pair it with what you now do about it. Rewrite these in your own words before using them.

How far they will dig

Deciding — Instinct

  1. What was that decision based on?
  2. Did you consider the alternatives?
  3. Why did you rule them out?

How to hold your ground

One reason for ruling something out is enough. Never say you didn't consider it — say you assessed it and set it aside. Not choosing is still a decision.

Working — Together

  1. What have you achieved alone?
  2. How far did you get without help?
  3. Why didn't you bring anyone in there?

How to hold your ground

“There was nobody to ask” sounds weak. “I judged that this part was mine to carry” turns the same fact into initiative.

Timing — Early

  1. When has a plan not held?
  2. What did you do first?
  3. Why that first?

How to hold your ground

The third question is about how you prioritise. Say what you dropped, not just what you did — that is where the judgment shows.

Standing — Backbone

  1. Tell me about taking initiative
  2. Did someone ask you to start?
  3. Why didn't you move before being asked?

How to hold your ground

“I was watching how it went” sounds passive. Say you were waiting for the conditions, or checking the ground first — frame the waiting as a decision.

Interviewers dig three levels into the same story. Candidates come apart at the second and third. Prepare these and you won't.

How to size up a company

Conditions that suit you

  • Few approval layers, and speed is what gets rewarded
  • Team-first by default, with people close enough to ask
  • Plans are expected to hold, and interruptions are rare
  • Defined roles, so expertise actually compounds

Ask them this

「How long does it usually take for a decision to land?」

How to read their answer

○“Usually on the spot.”

△“It depends on the case.” — a dodge. Follow with “what about the most recent one?”

×“About two weeks, it goes through sign-off.” — your speed will not count here

「How many people usually work on one thing?」

How to read their answer

○They give a real number: “two or three of us on anything”

△“Mostly solo, but you can ask” — probably solo in practice

×“Everyone sits apart and works their own way” — little room to pull people in

「How often do plans get thrown out mid-week?」

How to read their answer

○“The quarterly plan mostly holds.”

△“We pride ourselves on being flexible” — your early finishes may be wasted

×They dodge the question about crunch — that is the answer

「What role would I actually start in?」

How to read their answer

○They name the role and its scope without hesitating

△“You’ll get to try lots of things” — unclear what you would become

×“A bit of everything” — expertise will not compound

Warning signs

  • Long talk about sign-off. “We'd need a precedent for that.”
  • Everyone works solo. Nobody can see what anyone else is doing.
  • They dodge the question about crunch, and keep saying “flexible”.
  • “You'll do a bit of everything.” The role can't be described.

These double as your questions for them. Judge the fit yourself from the answers — a bad fit is not a bad company.
